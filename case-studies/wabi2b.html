Project overview

Wabi2b is Coca-Cola's B2B marketplace: a platform where wholesalers and distributors reach the independent stores in their region, and where store owners restock online instead of through a rep or a paper order. Working with the MyCoke product team, I led strategy and visual design to rebrand and redesign the shopping experience, add a seller-facing admin portal, and introduce a customer-service and equipment portal for stores.

The problem

Wholesalers needed a digital platform to upload their products so that stores could shop with different sellers at once. Stores, in turn, needed to compare prices easily, quickly, and online. The brief had three parts:

  • Rebrand and redesign the shopping experience for Wabi2b.
  • Give wholesalers a backend portal to adjust prices and add new products to their catalog.
  • Create an equipment and customer-service portal for stores.

Users & research

During the research phase we drew on prior qualitative research from the MyCoke product team — over 30 interviews and focus groups — to understand the challenges stores faced with the current Wabi2b experience. We asked what issues they faced, what to improve most, and what features they wanted next.

Key insights

  • Other platforms offer bulk-order deals — would Wabi2b bring those benefits?
  • Slow load speeds were a recurring frustration.
  • Quick reordering mattered more than discovery.
  • Stores wanted a mobile app and better responsive breakpoints for tablet and mobile.
  • Equipment repair required a phone call — could it be handled through the online portal?

Strategy & approach

I was responsible for both strategy and visual design. We moved through a Discover → Define → Ideate → Design → Test process: observations, informal chats, and user research up front; workshops, stakeholder meetings, and an opportunity map to define; an empathy map, information architecture, and user flows to ideate; then wireframes, UI/visual design, and prototypes, validated with usability testing and user feedback.

Design process

Define — empathy map

We built an empathy map to illustrate what current and future customers say, think, do, and feel — from "no product comparison" and "saved payments for faster ordering" to uncertainty about reliability and excitement about new ratings.

Ideate — IA & user flows

We defined the information architecture and user flows: landing, onboarding, verification, home, account, catalog, product details, shopping lists, reorder, order builder, checkout, delivery dates, promotions, minimum orders, payment details, and an equipment-repair/customer-service path.

Design — wireframes to hi-fi

The design phase produced desktop wireframes, hi-fi designs, and prototypes across browse, product detail, cart, checkout, shopping lists, and the equipment-service screens — plus a set of marketing how-to videos for the FAQ section (logging in for current MyCoke customers, understanding link expiry, updating shopping lists, and placing an order).

Outcome & impact

What shipped: a rebranded, redesigned shopping experience, a seller admin portal, an equipment/customer-service portal, and FAQ how-to videos — delivered as finished designs and prototypes over a 16-week engagement spanning 200+ screens.

In the team's words, this case study represents the start of a larger design process for Coca-Cola's future B2B e-commerce platforms; the goal was to explore more ways to bring additional customers onto the platform, and the work became the base template for other platforms built for the brand.

How I'd measure it

  • Order completion and time-to-reorder for returning stores.
  • Adoption of self-service equipment repair vs. phone support.
  • Load-speed improvements against the prior experience.
  • Mobile/tablet conversion after the responsive redesign.

What I'd do next · Reflections

Wabi2b is the kind of brief I like: a clear business goal, real user research to anchor it, and enough scope to design the whole system — storefront, seller tools, and service. The through-line to my later commerce work is the same instinct I'd carry into Cisco: respect the habit of the person reordering, and remove the friction around it. If I revisited it, I'd put a measurement plan against those insights from week one so the redesign's impact could be proven, not just shipped.
